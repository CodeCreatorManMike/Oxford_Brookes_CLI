1. Data alone is not enough
• Consider learning a Boolean function of 100 variables from a million
examples. There are 2100 − 106 examples whose classes you don’t know.
• How do you figure out what those classes are?
• In the absence of further information, there is just no way to do this that
beats flipping a coin.
• Every learner must embody some knowledge or assumptions beyond the
data it’s given in order to generalize beyond it.
• No learner can beat random guessing over all possible functions to be
learned.
Source: Pedro Domingos

2. Before you start to build models
• Take the time to understand your data
• Make sure you have enough data (cross-validation, augmentation)
• Talk to domain experts (understand which problems are useful to solve,
choose the most appropriate feature set and ML model to use, publish to
the most appropriate audience)
• Survey the literature (what has and what has not being done previously)
• Think about how your model will be deployed (resource-limited
environment (sensor, robot) may place limitations on the complexity of the
model, or there are time constraints, e.g. a classification of a signal is
required within milliseconds)

3. Overfitting that has many faces
• When your learner outputs a classifier that
is 100% accurate on the training data but
only 50% accurate on test data, when in
fact it could have output one that is 75%
accurate on both, it has overfit.
• One way to understand overfitting is by
decomposing generalization error into bias
and variance.
• Bias is a learner’s tendency to consistently
learn the same wrong thing.
• Variance is the tendency to learn random
things irrespective of the real signal.
Source: Pedro Domingos

4. How to reliably build models
• Don't allow test data to leak into the training process
• Try out a range of different models
• Consider combinations of models
• Don't use inappropriate models
• Optimise your model's hyperparameters
• Evaluate model multiple times
• Don't use accuracy with imbalanced data sets
• Consider using Cohen's kappa coefficient or Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC)
for imbalance data sets

6. Once we produce the model or report
• Do we just use it and go on?
• No, it’s not the end.
• How do we refine it over time?
• What if something isn’t quite right – can we debug it? justify it in an audit?
• What if we want to repeat the steps to build the model, over new training data?
• In terms of GDPR, someone might opt out of consenting for their data to
be used – how do we guarantee we aren’t using their data?
• Can we reuse the model or the report to evaluate another hypothesis?
